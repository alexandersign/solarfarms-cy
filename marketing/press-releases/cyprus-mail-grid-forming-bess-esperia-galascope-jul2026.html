Cyprus is finally building batteries — on parks that already waste sunshine

EMS vs BMS, grid-forming vs following, peak shaving, September’s schemes — and solar ROI in the hybrid era.

By Alexander Papacosta · Cyprus Director, Lighthief Cyprus Ltd · July 2026

On a clear spring midday in Cyprus, the sun does its job and the grid often does not. Solar parks generate clean power — then get told to switch off. Rooftop inverters drop offline. Evening comes, demand rises, and the island burns imported fuel again. Everyone pays somehow: on the bill, in carbon cost, or in watching free energy thrown away.

That story is familiar. What is new is that the metal is arriving — and with it, a vocabulary that used to live only in engineering rooms.

Batteries where the waste happens

This summer’s headlines belonged to public projects: 120 MW of Transmission System Operator batteries, the Electricity Authority’s Dhekelia system, the minister’s promise of “several hundred megawatts” by end-2027. An isolated island needs a backbone. But backbone alone will not stop every park spilling sunshine at noon. You also need batteries on the parks themselves.

That quieter story is moving. Lighthief Cyprus has locked in supply with Linyang — Power Atlantic systems we distribute exclusively here — and is advancing installations with Esperia Energy Group, including Galascope, across working solar parks: eleven parks, roughly 80 MW of solar, more than 300 MWh of storage. Parks that already produce, already curtail, and will now have somewhere to put the surplus.

“Esperia and Galascope were not looking for a brochure. Their parks lose energy on sunny days. We are putting batteries next to that problem — with Linyang’s equipment and a Cyprus team that has to make them work for twenty years, not for a ribbon-cutting.” — Alexander Papacosta, Cyprus Director, Lighthief Cyprus Ltd

BMS vs EMS

Every serious battery has two brains. Confusing them is how buyers get sold the wrong thing.

The BMS (Battery Management System) lives inside the container. It watches cell voltage and temperature, balances packs, tracks health, and trips the system before it cooks itself — the nurse on the ward.

The EMS (Energy Management System) sits above the hardware and decides what the battery should do for the business and the grid: charge now, discharge at 18:00, hold reserve, talk to the DSO — air-traffic control for timing, money and network rules.

A brilliant BMS with a dumb EMS is a safe brick. A clever EMS without a trustworthy BMS is a liability. Cyprus Category B hybrids need both.

Grid-following vs grid-forming

Most people hear “battery” and picture storage. On an island with no cable to anywhere, the sharper question is whether the inverter can help keep the grid steady.

Grid-following locks onto voltage and frequency that something else already creates — usually a conventional plant — and injects or absorbs current. Excellent passenger; needs the orchestra playing.

Grid-forming can create its own voltage and frequency reference. In virtual synchronous generator mode it behaves more like a spinning machine: synthetic inertia, black-start potential, the ability to steady a weak island grid. It can be the conductor.

On systems we bring through Linyang, that difference is largely firmware, not a different container. Plants can connect in following mode first, then switch to forming when DSO and TSO frameworks allow. Storage that only shifts kilowatt-hours is useful; storage that can compete with oil-fired machines for stability is transformative.

“Put storage where the waste happens, and let it earn its keep on the grid. Public batteries matter. Private ones on real parks matter too — because that is where the sunshine is actually being thrown away.” — Dr Arkadius Sybaris, Founder & CEO, Lighthief

Peak shaving

Peak shaving is unglamorous — and it pays bills.

For a solar park: midday, the DSO says stop exporting; the battery charges from panels that would otherwise be curtailed; between about 17:00 and 21:00, when prices spike, it discharges. Day-ahead data has shown midday wholesale prices around €100/MWh and evening peaks nearer €180–200. That spread is why co-located storage recovers energy already paid for in panels and land.

For a hotel, warehouse or factory: cut expensive spikes on the EAC bill. Charge from the rooftop, discharge into your own loads at peak, and stop buying the most painful kilowatt-hours. Same physics. Different cash register.

September’s schemes

Hardware without policy is a parked Ferrari. This autumn the two overlap more visibly.

Save-Upgrade Houses opens in September: €20 million, grants up to €32,000 per house for upgrades that can include photovoltaics, insulation, heating and cooling — another push for households to treat the roof as an energy asset.

Cyprus has also launched a competitive €35 million scheme to add storage beside existing renewable plants — the public twin of private programmes like Esperia–Galascope. And under net billing, exported midday power is worth less than self-consumed evening power. Batteries stop being optional.

Homeowners: read September’s fine print for your tariff and building age. Park owners: curtailment is already on the invoice.

Solar ROI in the hybrid era

Solar-only pitches cracked when curtailment rose toward the mid-forties per cent on hard days. Returns thinned precisely when the island needed clean power most.

For co-located utility projects we model for park owners, indicative returns typically sit at 8–13% annually with paybacks around 7–10 years, depending on curtailment, tariffs and financing. For commercial rooftops chasing bill reduction, well-matched sites often see paybacks in the low-to-mid single-digit years.

Those are directions, not promises. In Cyprus today, solar without storage is a half-finished sentence. Solar with an honest BMS, a timed EMS, and — when rules allow — grid-forming capability is how the asset earns its keep after the ribbon is cut.
